CANTONMENT LIFE AND COLONIAL ORDER IN BRITISH PUNJAB: A STUDY OF LAHORE CANTONMENT (1849–1947)
Author: Nida Maqsood (Nida Amir Afridi)
Degree: M.Phil. in History
Supervisor: Prof. Dr. Naumana Kiran
Department: Department of History and Pakistan Studies, University of the Punjab, Lahore
ORCID: 0009-0008-3608-2956
Abstract
This study examines cantonment life and colonial order in British Punjab through the case of Lahore Cantonment (originally established as Mian Mir) from 1849 to 1947. Colonial rule in Punjab cannot be understood solely through annexation, warfare, or provincial administration. It requires a broader understanding of the cantonment system, where everyday life was spatialised and regulated to maintain imperial authority. Drawing on Michel Foucault’s concepts of discipline, surveillance, and spatial control alongside David Arnold’s framework on colonial medicine and public health, this research analyses how the British used legal instruments—including the Military Cantonments Act of 1864, the Cantonments Acts of 1889 and 1924, and the Contagious Diseases Act of 1868—to govern barracks, bazaars, sanitation, civilian labour, gender relations, and mobility. Using colonial legislation, sanitary reports, archival records, and historical literature, the thesis demonstrates that Lahore Cantonment functioned not merely as a military base, but as a stratified socio-administrative system that structured disparities of race, rank, class, and gender in daily life.
Thesis Structure
• Introduction: Theoretical framework, literature review, research gap, and methodology.
• Chapter 1 – Punjab: A Garrison Province, Cantonments and Colonial Rules: Examines the 1849 annexation, martial race recruitment policies, the purpose of cantonments, and civil–military relations.
• Chapter 2 – Lahore Cantonment: Planning, Law, and the Making of a Military Landscape: Traces the shift from Anarkali to Mian Mir, early land surveys, the impact of the 1857 Uprising, legal consolidation (1864, 1889, 1924 Acts), architectural hierarchy, and infrastructure development.
• Chapter 3 – Daily Life at Lahore Cantonment: Explores barrack routines, social composition, the dependent civilian and bazaar economy, gender and lock hospitals, epidemic disease control (cholera and malaria), transport networks, and leisure culture.
• Conclusion: Key findings and avenues for future comparative research on colonial and post-1947 cantonments.
